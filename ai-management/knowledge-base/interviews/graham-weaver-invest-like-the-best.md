# How Graham Weaver Turns Recent Grads Into CEOs

> **Source:** YouTube / Invest Like the Best (Patrick O'Shaughnessy) — "How Graham Weaver Turns Recent Grads Into CEOs" (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u2nHljZeMQ8)
> **Speaker:** Graham Weaver (Founder & CEO, Alpine Investors)
> **Interviewer:** Patrick O'Shaughnessy

## Alpine's Three North Stars

1. **Be the #1 performing PE fund in the world** as measured by MOIC. Specific goal: **5x on every fund**. Last four funds have achieved or are on track for 5x.
2. **Be the best place to work** for top talent
3. **Use the platform as a force for good**

The 5x target emerged during the Great Recession when Alpine had no fund for years and spent three years doing deep thinking with executive coaches. Initially targeted 3x (enough to be "in the conversation"), but consistently outperformed and raised the bar.

> "Make no little plans, for they have no power to stir one's blood." — Daniel Burnham

## Origin Story: 12 Years Old in Ohio

Graham grew up in a small Rust Belt town outside Toledo. Parents went through a bitter divorce. Changed schools, no friends. Mowed lawns six hours every weekend.

**The Sony Walkman turning point:** Used a Walkman to listen to self-help audiobooks from the library while mowing:
- *Think and Grow Rich* — Napoleon Hill
- *Lead the Field* — Earl Nightingale
- *Universal Laws of Success and Achievement* — Brian Tracy

### Three foundational concepts from thousands of hours of lawnmowing:

1. **You don't get to be a victim.** Take control of your life.
2. **Decide what you want and write down your goals.** Graham wrote his goals 3-4 times a day throughout high school.
3. **Make choices.** You can have any goal, but not 20. Pick 1-2 you're excited about and give yourself permission to not pursue goals 3 through 30.

### The Wrestling Story: Choosing to Suffer

First major application of these principles. Sophomore year, varsity wrestling at 155 lbs. The captain dropped to his weight class. The only spot available was at 125 lbs. Graham, 6 feet tall, **dropped 30 pounds** — eating 900 calories a day, running in 9-degree weather.

> "If I could do that, at least other goals I set, I was able to eat."

This gave him a visceral experience of choosing to change the trajectory of his life.

> Dan Gable quote: "Once you've wrestled, everything in life is easy."

## Princeton: Three Impossible Goals

Felt "unshackled" — like Forrest Gump when his braces come off. Set three goals:
1. Be valedictorian
2. Be the #1 rower in the United States (had never rowed before; didn't know boats went backwards)
3. Start a business to pay for school

**Didn't achieve any of them, but came close on all three.**

### The Rowing Story

- Met Mike Tety (later coached US Olympic team to gold) who was the only other person there. After 3 weeks of Graham showing up, Tety took him under his wing.

## Wall Street: 3% of Capacity

Recruited by Morgan Stanley into PE in 1994. Learned to build a financial model in three weeks, then did that for two years at 80-100 hours/week.

> "Is this what work is like? I've been in school 16 years and this is my big moment and it was incredibly disappointing."

## Starting Alpine

Got into Stanford GSB. Decided to buy companies out of his dorm room. Named it "Alpine" (started with "A" for the Yellow Pages, he lived on Alpine Road).

No class on Wednesdays — would take a red-eye Tuesday night to visit tiny manufacturing businesses in the Midwest, then fly back for exams.

**Bought three companies at age 25.** Financed them creatively:
- EBITDA of ~約7,500万円（$500K）each, purchased for ~約3億円（$2M）
- Seller financed ~約1.5億円（$1M）
- Equipment loans, bank financing
- Got equity down to ~約1,500万円（$100K）
- Raised small amounts ($10K, $5K) from Wall Street contacts
- **Used Capital One promotional checks** ("Write yourself a check for $25,000, no interest for 2 years") for his equity contribution

> "I was playing a very high-wire act, which I do not recommend."

### Results: 1x on first three deals

The three label printing companies collectively returned 1x. Mistakes:
1. **Didn't underwrite the underlying customers** — When recession hit in 2001, Midwest clients got destroyed
2. **Wildly underappreciated how much management matters** — Backed the #2 person after founder retired. Made this mistake three times.

**Fourth deal** (same industry): Biggest customer was **Trader Joe's** — grew 15% annually, paid in 5 days, great margins. Owned it for 22 years. Dividends helped fund early Alpine.

### The Leap

Even knowing exactly what he wanted, Graham chickened out. Took a job at an institutional PE fund. "A little bit of me died the day I started."

A close friend his age passed away. He quit with nothing to land on — no fund, no investors, nothing.

## The "Winnable Game" Philosophy

### Peter Thiel Model vs. Schwarzenegger Model
- **Thiel:** Sit, think, make infrequent decisions based on special insight
- **Schwarzenegger:** Brute force, more reps, more push

Alpine's approach: **Use 25% of energy finding a winnable game, then 75% on brute-force execution.**

### The Red Ocean Problem
5,500 PE funds. For a high-quality ERP subscription software business, it goes to the highest bidder — not the smartest. That's a non-winnable game.

### Alpine's Winnable Game
Find a ~約30億円（$20M）revenue plumbing company in Ball, Louisiana where the owner's retiring and needs a new management team. Nobody wants to sign up for that game at scale.

> "The investment committee decision is not the hard part. You're paying 8x EBITDA, with the playbook you can blend down to 5x, borrow at 5.5%, and your platform trades at 18x. The hard part is getting the CEO in place and IT systems."

## The Talent Model: How CIT Was Born

### The Discovery
In Alpine's early days, when businesses weren't going well, the partners themselves would go run them. Dan Sander moved to Detroit for a year. Will Adams moved to Maine for two years. Graham ran a slot machine business.

Looking back during the recession: **the best deals were always the ones where they put in someone like themselves** — no industry experience, but raw talent and coachability. Those people would run through walls and look at things fresh.

> "At one point we just said, 'Let's just do that every time.'"

### Burning the Boats (2010)
Decision: install their own management team 100% of the time. This required:
- Rebuilding the entire sourcing engine (bankers don't sell companies without management teams)
- Changing their market brand ("If you don't want to continue, you call Alpine")

### The Inauspicious Start
Graham was a guest lecturer at Stanford GSB. Students wanted to be CEOs but didn't like the search fund model. He invited one student to join Alpine.

**Couldn't place the first person.** Portfolio CEOs said: "You've got someone with no experience, who's entitled, and super expensive. How about no."

Second year: Laura Walsh. Graham offered a **money-back guarantee** — "If she doesn't work out in a year, I'll reimburse her salary." She knocked it out of the park. The CEO said: "I want three more."

Growth: Year 1 → 1 person. Year 2 → 2. Year 3 → 3. Took a long time to figure out what kind of businesses they'd succeed in.

**Today:** Alpine CIT is the #1 most applied-to position at Harvard Business School, Stanford GSB, and Kellogg.

## The Four Key Attributes

1. **White-hot will to win.** "I'm going to put this on my shoulders and run it through this burning building." Must show up historically in their life.
2. **Grit.** Been knocked down, got up. Repeatedly. "More rare than you would think at some of the schools we recruit from."
3. **Emotional intelligence / Self-awareness.** When Alpine has had big failures, this is the one they missed.
4. **Bias for action** (the surprising one). Getting in the arena, trying stuff, failing, trying again — versus analysis paralysis.

## Platform Building 101: The Apex Example

1. **Pick a CIT pair:** AJ Brown (CIT → CFO at a portfolio company → CEO) paired with Will Matson (incoming CIT). CEO + CFO.
2. **Visit 20+ companies** in the industry. Each company does one thing great. Aggregate all best practices → best playbook in the world.
3. **Architect the playbook before writing a check.**
4. **Buy small first:** First deal ~約12億円（$8M）EBITDA. Learn. Buy another at ~約3億円（$2M）EBITDA. Build along the way.
5. **Overinvest in the hold co** (CEO, CFO, Chief People Officer). Some young platforms have ~約22.5億円（$15M）hold co expense — funded by the fund, quickly offset by portfolio company EBITDA.
6. **Find the winnable sub-game:** All HVAC competitors buy $5-10M EBITDA businesses with great management. 90% of the market is below that — management wants to leave. Nobody else is buying there.
7. **Talent as lever:** Apex tapped the military veteran market, built their own CIT program. 80 military veterans as GMs. Brad Schwarz (West Point → Green Beret → Wharton) went from running ~約12億円（$8M）revenue business to a ~約750億円（$500M）division.

**Result:** ~約75億円（$50M）of equity in → ~約750億円（$500M）of EBITDA out. No additional equity invested.

## The CEO Day Zero Playbook

Alpine has ~30 coaches versed in their playbook who pair with first-time CEOs for a paint-by-numbers first six months.

### Day Zero: The Announcement
- Employees are upset. Joe's Plumbing — Joe's retiring after 15 years. Here's a 28-year-old who doesn't know the industry.

### First 60 Days: Listen
Meet with 20-30 people. Ask:
- What do you do here?
- What's going well? What else? What else?
- What's not going well? What else?
- If you were me, what would you focus on?
- What are the top priorities?
- What are we working on that's a waste of time?
- What should I be worried about?
- Biggest opportunities? Biggest problems?

> "Without fail, one of the first things people say is: 'I've worked here for 15 years and no one's ever asked me my opinion before.'"

### Then: Enroll Top People in Designing the Plan
Acknowledge what you heard. You don't have to do everything they say, but they have to feel heard. The low-hanging fruit is usually "so low and so obvious."

> "The answer is always in the room. You don't have to go hire McKinsey. If you hire McKinsey, they're going to go interview your people and repeat back what you just heard."

### Leading Indicators
Two most underappreciated leading indicators of success:
1. **Employee NPS** — measured at acquisition, then every 6 months, published across all of Alpine
2. **Customer NPS**

> "40,000 employees are having an experience where they enjoy coming to work significantly more after we buy the business. That's probably the thing I'm proudest of."

## Financial Architecture of Returns

### Individual Deal Underwriting
- Target: **3x gross in 5 years** (no multiple expansion assumed)
- Buy, leverage, grow

### Fund-Level 5x
- Achieved through **asymmetric outcomes** — organic growth better than expected, hold longer
- Portfolio manage your way to 5x through getting on base consistently plus outlier deals
- If you underwrote individual deals to 5x, you'd never close a deal

### The Outlier Pattern
Like Buffett making half his money on two stocks (GEICO + Washington Post). Alpine tends to have at least one outlier per fund.

Ingredients of the outlier: **Build a real company with a real hold co, phenomenal team, in a large industry. Spend time getting the playbook right. Give yourself breathing room.**

Most recent fund: nearly every company has outlier potential.

## Stanford GSB Class: "Managing Growing Enterprises"

### Irv Grousbeck's Influence
Took over Irv Grousbeck's class (father of search funds). Irv was "one of the most influential people in my life — and probably literally a thousand people would say the same thing."

Irv's method: Takes notes after every conversation, reviews before the next meeting. Always the voice of "you've got this."

Graham was a case guest in Irv's class for 12 years — as "the token failure case."

### Class Evolution

**Years 1-4:** Taught CEO skills — hiring, firing, difficult conversations. Role-playing actual conversations at the "one-foot level." Students loved it, but didn't act on their dreams.

**Year 5+:** Added 25% of curriculum on personal growth:
- Helping students figure out what they really want
- Visualization exercises
- Journaling
- Confronting limiting beliefs and designing a path forward

### Key Exercises

**Nine Lives Exercise:** Instead of "find your one passion" (intimidating), say "If you had nine lives, what would you do in each?" Students rattle them off instantly. Then: "Good news — your thing is in there somewhere." Dig into which ones have the most energy.

> "The secret is you can have all nine at some point in your life, just not all at the same time."

**20-Year Visualization:** Meet your future self 20 years from now. They've been incredibly successful. What does that look like? What advice does that future self give you?

**The Genie Question:** "What would you do if you knew you wouldn't fail?"

### How Teaching Changed Graham
- 12th year teaching; spends more time preparing than in year one
- Forces him to codify his latest thinking on business, personal growth, spirituality
- **Accountability mirror:** Can't teach something he's not practicing himself

## Executive Coaching: Personal Growth Defined

### Graham's Three Coaches

1. **Mandy Shoemaker** (Tony Robbins system) — Weekly accountability. Before each call: "What are your 1-year goals? What did you say you'd do last week? What did you do? What will you do this week?"
2. **Blue-sky coach** — 4-hour sessions of messy thinking. "What do we want Alpine to be 10 years from now? If you achieved your 10-year goals in 6 months, what would be true?"
3. **Rachel Lockett** — Org chart and people strategy. Has been CPO at large companies. Guides Graham through scaling challenges he's never faced before.

### Personal Growth = Listening to Intuition + Having Courage to Act

> "Each of us has most of the answers inside us, in our intuition. It's like our own LLM — it's taken every experience and input we've ever had and stored it all. Personal growth is understanding what that intuition is telling you, getting out of your head, and having the courage to go do what it says."

### Questions to Access Intuition
- Meditation, breathwork, stillness
- "What would I do if I wasn't afraid?"
- "How is fear playing into my life right now?"
- Carl Jung: **"Where your fear is, there is your task."**
- "If your 10-years-older self gave you advice right now, what would they say?"

## The Worst Deal: Slot Machines

Bought a slot machine manufacturing/software business. On paper good: recurring revenue (20% rev share), sold to Native American tribes during gaming expansion.

Problems:
- **Massive concentration risk:** 約100億円（$68M）fund, 約255億円（$170M）in the business including co-invest. Too big to fail.
- Technology risk, customer concentration, capex, product obsolescence
- Industry had "people that weren't playing by the rules"
- Graham became CEO, partner became CFO

**The 5-year-old girl moment:** Walking into a local casino, seeing a child with a coloring book while her mom was inside spending her paycheck. "I don't want to be in this business."

**The escape:** Anticipated Illinois legalizing slot machines in bars. Made deals with bars years ahead of competitors. When the law passed, Alpine was the largest or second largest supplier. Sold the business. Initial equity ~3x, late equity ~1x. Everyone got paid.

**Lessons:** Don't bet the firm on one company. Back businesses where you're having a positive impact.

## The PE Industry's Future

### 30-Year Tailwind (1990-2020)
- 10-year Treasury went from 8% to 0%
- Pension funds underwrote at 8-9%, had to find alternatives → PE allocations went from 0% to ~20%
- Cheap debt → multiple expansion. Same companies bought at 5x in 1994 now cost 13x.

### Today
- 5,500 funds, highly efficient market
- Interest rates going the other way
- New dynamic: individual investors accessing PE through wealth management
- Median-return funds that assumed they could always raise bigger → "not going to work out well in the next decade"
- Must be differentiated or be a mega-firm collecting individual capital

## What Bothers Graham About PE

> "If you watch what firms do — not what they say — their objective statement is: go raise the next fund, go raise a bigger fund."

This leads to wrong behaviors: "Cut your flowers and water your weeds." Sell best companies quickly to show returns and raise the next fund. Compound the stuff that's not great.

## The Duration Truth

- 21 years into the industry: managed ~約600億円（$400M）, huge team, **salary was $100,000**
- No carry check yet (European waterfall, waiting for best company to exit)

> "Pick something you're excited about that you want to stay with for a long time, because if you're in it to make money and exit, you're probably going to be disappointed."

### The Dirty Secret
> "The real part of your life is the journey — the building, the not knowing how it's going to turn out, the challenges. That's all the fun stuff. What's happening along the way is you're growing and learning who you are. That's actually what it's really about."

Alan Watts: **"The point of dancing is not to get to a particular spot on the dance floor."**

## Graham's Time Allocation Today

- **75%** working on Alpine itself (talent, culture, recruiting, career advancement)
- **25%** in the action (deals, boards) — to stay connected to real problems

> "I worked at four PE firms before Alpine. The leader of every single one looked at their job as being a deal person. They spent virtually no time saying: how do I make this the place where the best people want to stay?"

## Relevance to Taiga

### Investment Strategy Insights
1. **5x MOIC as objective function** — not IRR. Prevents the "cut your flowers, water your weeds" behavior of selling winners early to raise the next fund
2. **3x individual deal underwriting → 5x fund through asymmetric outliers** — a replicable portfolio construction philosophy
3. **B+ industry + A+ team > A+ industry + B team** — directly applicable to Taiga's Japanese market deal sourcing
4. **Winnable game framework:** 25% of energy finding the right game, 75% on brute-force execution within it
5. **Platform building playbook:** Visit 20+ companies → aggregate best practices → architect playbook before first check → overinvest in hold co

### Talent & Operations Insights
6. **Day Zero CEO playbook** — 60-day listening protocol is immediately actionable for any portfolio company management transition
7. **Employee NPS as leading indicator** — measured at acquisition, published across portfolio, CEO accountability
8. **CIT origin story:** Started with a money-back guarantee on one person. Scale came from delivering on the promise, not marketing.
9. **Four attributes (Will to Win, Grit, EQ, Bias for Action)** — a structured hiring framework for portfolio company leadership
10. **Military veteran talent pipeline** — Apex's use of veterans as GMs is a creative talent arbitrage

### Personal & Organizational
11. **21 years at $100K salary** — the ultimate test of passion alignment. Duration > short-term compensation.
